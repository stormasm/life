

Cormac McCarthy’ Review: A Novelist’s Long Road

The author was his own myth-maker, claiming that, as a boy, he never left home without a gun.

By Bill Heavey   
Oct. 2, 2026    
10:08 am ET



When I first read “All the Pretty Horses” 30 years ago, it took the top of my head off. The first of three novels in Cormac McCarthy’s Border Trilogy, it seemed, with its torrential power, to come out of nowhere. Its tone and rhythms are oracular and epic, as if some ancient prophet is buttonholing you to tell you how the world has always been and will always be. Sure, it owes a debt to Faulkner—with its high diction and sentences that outpace their punctuation—but it isn’t imitating him. McCarthy said that “books are made out of books,” and his sure are: Hawthorne, Hemingway, Joyce, Melville, Wolfe and others. The vocabulary is obscure and arcane. There are no quotation marks and few commas.


McCarthy would later say you don’t need punctuation if you write well. He wrote better than well. To this day I can’t shake a three-sentence description of birds driven onto cholla thorns by the winds of a thunderstorm. “Gray nameless birds espaliered in attitudes of stillborn flight. . . . Some of them were still alive as they twisted on their spines as the horses passed and raised their heads and cried out but the horsemen rode on.” It’s the McCarthy cosmos in a nutshell: The good news is that the world is not random chaos but rather ordered by an unseen power, the bad is that this power is completely indifferent and unknowable. McCarthy’s novels—like all human endeavor—were doomed before they were even written, but they are still stupendous.


As Tracy Daugherty details in his engrossing “Cormac McCarthy: A Legacy Revisited,” the overnight success of “All the Pretty Horses” (1992) was decades in coming. Leaving his native Knoxville, Tenn., in the mid-1970s to head west, McCarthy trailed behind him “two failed marriages and three novels largely ignored by the public.” Largely ignored is right. “The Orchard Keeper” (1965), “Outer Dark” (1968) and “Child of God” (1973) collectively sold less than 10,000 copies. Critics complained about the author’s “thesauritis,” his preoccupation with violence and what one critic called his “hostility toward the reader.”


Almost performatively indifferent to fame, McCarthy instead tended to the myth, nurtured all his life, that he was a loner and outsider. He claimed that, as a boy, “I never left the house without a gun. Later on, I made my own rifles.” This may be true, but it’s definitely something a myth-maker would say. He also carried a 100-watt lightbulb everywhere to ensure good reading light.
From youth McCarthy had, by all accounts, exceptional observation skills and intelligence. He seemed to be an expert at everything, including astronomy, architecture, acting, cars, taxidermy, music, masonry and whales. He was drawn to people who worked without a net. “Outlaws” is how he described people like himself and his friend Betty Carey, a professional poker player. “We’re desperate people.” One intimate claimed McCarthy knew “every gambler, hustler, pool shark, and bad check artist” in east Tennessee. “And they were all friends of his.”

Mr. Daugherty points out that “Blood Meridian” (1985), now seen as one of McCarthy’s best novels, was initially faulted by one critic for its “strained surrealism.” Another simply called it “tiresome.” But the tide was turning. Harold Bloom wrote that “no other living American novelist . . . has given us a book as strong and memorable. . . . It is the ultimate western, not to be surpassed.” People were starting to notice something else. “Somehow Mr. McCarthy is able to hold firm to his pessimism while allowing the reader to see beyond it,” one critic wrote. “This is art that both frightens and inspires.”

Mr. Daugherty, the author of critically acclaimed biographies of Larry McMurtry, Joan Didion and Donald Barthelme, deftly separates the writer from the myth, capturing McCarthy’s evolution from a Southern writer to a Western one with a biography that pulls you along like a novel. But he doesn’t shy away from the troubling details, including the nearly 50-year relationship McCarthy had with Augusta Britt.

The two met in 1976. She was 16 and he was 42. She had been in and out of foster homes for years and carried a revolver, determined that no man would ever hit her again. He greeted her by asking if she intended to shoot him. The romance eventually faded but the relationship continued until McCarthy’s death in 2023. In a 2024 Vanity Fair story after his death, the identity of his “secret muse” finally came out. “He was my safety,” Ms. Britt said. “I really feel that if I had not met him, I would have died young.” McCarthy kept the relationship secret and tried to make it look legal. “There’s nothing more romantic than watching a man forge your birth certificate,” Ms. Britt said.

Reaction was swift. “Let’s be honest with ourselves,” one critic wrote. “Cormac McCarthy groomed a teenage girl.” Another wrote: “It is impolite, and not a little bit arrogant, for feminists to tell such women that we know more about their lives than they do. But perhaps such arrogance is called for, especially amid the backdrop of a media world that is loudly announcing its intention to launder abuse.” Only time will tell how this relationship will affect McCarthy’s legacy, but, judging by the reputations of artists from Thomas Eakins to Pablo Picasso, Michael Jackson and Elvis Presley, the world has a high tolerance for the sexual predations of male artists. McCarthy’s greatest works, such as “Blood Meridian” and “The Road” (2006), are already considered American classics.

McCarthy believed that the unconscious mind knew things that the conscious mind didn’t. He didn’t like to talk about the process of writing. Instead, he liked to tell the story of an expert dove hunter who was asked one day if he shot with one or both eyes open. As McCarthy tells it, the hunter said he didn’t know, but that he would think about it. The man never hit another dove again.

—Mr. Heavey is the editor-at-large of Field & Stream magazine.
