
### Halloween 2026

This was written two weeks after this [reading by tracy](./tracy-reading.md)

By Sam Buntz

In Cormac McCarthy’s The Crossing, we encounter a moment of extreme violence. A blind man, a veteran of the 1910-1920 Mexican Revolution, recounts to the main character the suffering he experienced at the hands of a German mercenary in captivity:

The German then did something very strange. He smiled and licked the man’s spittle from about his mouth. He was a very large man with enormous hands and he reached and seized the young captive’s head in both these hands and bent as if to kiss him. But it was no kiss. He seized him by the face and it may well have looked to others that he bent to kiss him on each cheek perhaps in the military manner of the French but what he did instead with a great caving of his cheeks was to suck each in turn the man’s eyes from his head and spit them out again and leave them dangling by their cords wet and strange and wobbling on his cheeks. [...] He could see the trampled dust of the street beneath him. A crazed jumble of men’s boots. He could see his own mouth. [...] They tried to put his eyes back into their sockets with a spoon but none could manage it and the eyes dried on his cheeks like grapes and the world grew dim and colorless and then it vanished forever.

Usually, when called upon to give an example of McCarthy’s gift for depicting violence, people pick examples from the even more violent Blood Meridian. But this image from The Crossing stuck with me. There’s a poetic awfulness to the full passage, and it’s such a gruesome and bizarre thing to imagine. It almost makes me feel ill, yet I find myself awestruck by it. We have to ponder the nature of the genius that produced this. What was driving him? This Halloween, as we encounter ghosts, goblins, hatchet-wielding serial killers, zombies, and vampires, it would perhaps be appropriate to turn our attention to America’s foremost literary explorer of the problem of evil.

The scene is set by the release of Tracy Daugherty’s Cormac McCarthy: A Legacy Revisited, which leads us on an expansive tour of the writer’s external life. It is admirably well researched and, at times, written with considerable flair. We learn about McCarthy’s love affairs, his motley assortment of eccentric and often criminal Knoxville friends, his passion for automobiles, his sudden liberation from obscurity, his involvement with Hollywood, and much else. We learn about the profound difficulties of his relationship with his father and the lasting wounds of his failed first marriage. 

And yet … why do McCarthy’s books exist? Blood Meridian. The Road. Suttree. The Crossing. These are some of the most disturbing, haunting, and affecting works in late-20th- and early-21st- century American literature, and they’re the reason we’re interested in their creator. They smear evil right in your face, and you can’t help ingesting some of its grease. When McCarthy died, I remember a palpable sense that he was the last of his kind in America: a writer of truly mythic stature, like his forefathers Herman Melville and William Faulkner. 

But the question of McCarthy’s genius and where it came from isn’t raised by Daugherty’s text. We learn McCarthy’s preferences in men’s fashion, the fact that he was, at one point, a member of the Republican National Committee, the fact that he ran away with a friend’s wife to Texas, etc. And we learn more about the now-notorious fact that his relationship with his apparent muse, Augusta Britt, was consummated when she was 17 years old and McCarthy was 43. But none of this touches the actual reason any of us care about McCarthy: his books. Despite our thoroughly disenchanted age, there’s a dark miracle present in McCarthy’s work, and the biographer’s task is to attempt to explain it rather than explain it away. 

When I was in divinity school, I remember a gentle old lady classmate being freaked out by The Road (I suppose it was the baby-eating cannibals that did it). “I can’t imagine what it would be like to live with him!” she said, disturbed that such twisted visions could enter a human mind. I wanted to say that I routinely entertained thoughts about the possible consequences of civilizational collapse (speculatively and with a fair degree of detachment, and not specifically about cannibalism) at least five times before breakfast, but I held my tongue. And, to be fair, my own aptitude for imagining evil on a cosmic scale pales before McCarthy’s. 

Where did this darkest of dark visions come from? Like McCarthy, many people have had abusive and uncomprehending fathers. Many people have been divorced and tormented by guilt. But why was this man in particular the midwife of these novels? Daugherty’s biography fails to provide an answer to that question. It has illuminating comments to make about and connections to draw from the work, but we don’t get a sense of the white-hot heat of a mind in dialogue with itself—wrestling with evil, dabbling in evil, and turning away from it. We learn about the troubles McCarthy both suffered and instigated, but we don’t see his mind in process, taking on that trouble and transforming it. (And that was Thomas Carlyle’s definition of genius: “transcendent capacity of taking trouble.”) We don’t get a sense that some unusual power was at work here. But it was. 

If you read Richard Ellmann’s Yeats: The Man and the Masks, you can get a better sense of how minds ought to be captured. We come to understand the forces animating William Butler Yeats, feeding his soul and driving him to accomplish miraculous things with words. For instance, we become fully immersed in Yeats’ engagement with poets, the occult, magical rituals, Celtic mythology, theosophy, and Irish nationalism. We see those forces work their way into his poetry in detail, since Ellmann curates Yeats’ quotations and conversations to give us a sense of his continual artistic and intellectual progress, his quarrel with the world and with himself. But in Daugherty’s biography, we only get passing glimpses of the equivalent currents in McCarthy’s life and work.

For example, McCarthy was obsessed with Ludwig Wittgenstein. According to an article in the Smithsonian Magazine on Cormac’s massive library, Cormac’s brother, Dennis, said, “The human being whom McCarthy most admired [...] was Ludwig Wittgenstein.” McCarthy owned 142 books by or on the Austrian philosopher “with a high proportion annotated.” But Wittgenstein’s influence on McCarthy is mentioned only twice (and very briefly) in Daugherty’s biography. Why was McCarthy so entranced with Wittgenstein? His friend David Krakauer, the President of the Santa Fe Institute where McCarthy was a fellow, discussed this affinity in a profile for Nautilus:

In addition to Wittgenstein’s insights into language and meaning and his compressive style, Cormac told me how “everything that [Wittgenstein] thought about had to do with whether it was right or wrong and he worried about it constantly,” and “if philosophy is not concerned with the way one should live what the hell is it concerned with?”

There is much more that could be said about this, and the influence of Wittgenstein on Cormac McCarthy’s later moral vision seems important. For instance, in a 2009 Wall Street Journal interview, Cormac expanded on his sense of the spiritual life: 

I have a great sympathy for the spiritual view of life, and I think that it’s meaningful. But am I a spiritual person? I would like to be. Not that I am thinking about some afterlife that I want to go to, but just in terms of being a better person. I have friends at the Institute. They’re just really bright guys who do really difficult work solving difficult problems, who say, “It’s really more important to be good than it is to be smart.” And I agree it is more important to be good than it is to be smart. That is all I can offer you.

This sounds much like Wittgenstein’s own approach to spirituality: austere, unwilling to affirm or deny, concentrated strictly on morality. McCarthy’s emphasis on the persistence of goodness under ghastly conditions in The Road seems indebted to this perspective, as do many of the intriguing philosophical conversations in the Border Trilogy, which includes The Crossing. But since Daugherty is dismissive of the moral dimension of McCarthy’s work, we don’t hear much about any of this or learn about these connections. 

(There are other interesting omissions: for instance, Krakauer said in a recent interview that reading Richard Ellmann’s biography of James Joyce convinced McCarthy that he needed to become a novelist. But Joyce, a massive influence on Suttree, is mentioned by Daugherty only fleetingly). 

Daugherty does mention McCarthy’s interest in gnosticism, the ancient Christian heresy that declares that the creator of our world was a mostly incompetent bungler called the Demiurge—that is, not the true God. He also notes McCarthy’s religious struggles and doubts (though not in much detail). But what seems salient about McCarthy’s mind to me is the presence of a fierce, unrelenting spiritual vision, one struggling to reckon with a profound sense of our world as “a universe of death” (to borrow a phrase from John Milton, another explorer of sublimity and evil). Even more grimly, Alicia Western in Stella Maris proclaims that “Reality is loss.” Goodness is an outcast, a fugitive, wandering largely unrecognized through the abandoned ruins of our sunken universe, where “War is God.” This is a cosmos defined by Darwinian combat; the big fish eat the little ones. When the father and son in The Road tell each other they’re “carrying the fire,” they’re seeking to keep the flame of transcendent goodness burning in the midst of this wasteland. McCarthy, in other words, was possessed by a singular vision of evil (and of good), one certainly drawing on gnosticism, but one placed within hailing distance of religious orthodoxy as well. 

I fear that people are going to be put off from McCarthy by the biography—not only because of the already-known revelations about his relationship with Augusta Britt. There’s the suggestion that McCarthy may have committed credit card fraud when he was younger, that he slept with some of his friends’ wives in the sleazy and swinging ’70s, and so on and so forth. All this gives the impression of young McCarthy as louche and unscrupulous. But if you read other writings about McCarthy, like the aforementioned Smithsonian profile, you emerge with a different picture. He was someone constantly, feverishly engaged on an intellectual level and also, at least in later life, concerned with his moral condition and reformation. According to the Vanity Fair piece on his relationship with Britt, McCarthy once told his son John Francis, “The difference between you and me is that you were born a good person. I had to work hard to become one.”

This sense of moral struggle isn’t articulated in Daugherty’s book, but it should be; it’s fully available to the public. For example, the Wittliff Collections at Texas State University, which hold McCarthy’s papers, contain a list of McCarthy’s “goals.” These seem to be from later in life, I would guess, given that they appear to be from after he finally got rich. McCarthy warns himself against the “drive of acquisition,” equating it with desiring “more and more of the things worth having at the expense of the things worth being.” He also cautions himself, “Low self-esteem is fed by ambition and nourishes anger. […] Other people will not meet your standards. You want to feel contempt.” This self-chastening, clearly not a one-time thing, isn’t particularly evident in Daugherty’s biography. 

I think this sense of moral struggle is tied into McCarthy’s vision of evil. His consciousness seems caught between skepticism and a yearning for the greater life the Christian character in his play The Sunset Limited envisions: “[Jesus] said you could have life everlastin. Life. Have it today. Hold it in your hand. That you could see it. It gives off a light. It’s got a little weight to it. Not much. Warm to the touch. Just a little. And it’s forever. And you can have it. Now. Today. But you don’t want it. You don't want it cause to get it you got to let your brother off the hook.”

I think this moral struggle—dismissed as a search for “pat” answers by Daugherty—is intimately related to McCarthy’s genius, to his capacity to absorb and transform trouble and reckon with darkness inside and out. Moral effort should stand out even more brightly against one’s imperfections, just as the torch-bearing father-son duo of The Road makes goodness stand out more brightly against the background of bleak hopelessness and pervasive cruelty. Henrik Ibsen once said that “To live is to war with trolls in heart and soul. To write is to sit in judgement on oneself.” McCarthy’s life and work provide testament to this maxim.
